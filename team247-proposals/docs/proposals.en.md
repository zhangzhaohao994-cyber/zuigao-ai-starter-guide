# Highest × Team247: Three Product Proposals

Discussion draft V4 · November 7, 2026

Liang and Ting, I have made the three directions more concrete: a favorite identity as a personal life assistant; industry knowledge and processes turned into an Agent that delivers work; and visual novels made from personal stories, with sharing bringing in more creators.

I would contribute experience in branding and marketing, the aesthetics industry, art and storytelling, and building products with AI. I hope to work with the team on products that can run reliably, be delivered, and test revenue. The features below are proposed development goals. Channels, pricing, and distribution remain hypotheses; no customers, rights, or budgets are committed.

## Direction 1: Customize a favorite identity into an AI for everyday life

### 1. Product idea

**Let a person or character the user loves wake them up, remind them, and capture notes—with a familiar appearance, voice, and way of speaking.**

With the necessary rights in place, a user could choose a character inspired by an authorized public figure such as Jay Chou, or a favorite anime character. With that person's consent, they could also create an AI character based on a first love, partner, teacher, or classmate. The first thing they encounter is an identity they care about, rather than a standardized assistant.

Customization covers name, appearance, voice, tone, signature phrases, personality, backstory, forms of address, and interaction habits. For a personal character, users can describe how the person usually speaks, their favorite phrases, or how they might offer comfort. The system turns this material into an editable character profile.

The goal is consistency across text, voice, and daily reminders, creating the feeling that a familiar identity is part of everyday life. The product will clearly identify it as an AI character. Real people and commercial IP require appropriate consent and authorization; the experience must not imply that the actual person is participating.

### 2. Target users

**Start with devoted fans who feel strongly connected to a person, IP, or creator, then serve people who want to customize an important person in their own lives.**

The first group includes loyal audiences of musicians, anime characters, virtual personalities, and creators. Their interest already exists; the reason to buy is a more personal, everyday form of interaction. The second group wants a life assistant based on a consenting partner, teacher, or friend.

Wake-ups, reminders, and notes are the everyday situations in which that emotional connection becomes useful. What defines the audience is whom they care about and how they want to interact—not simply a need for an alarm clock.

We will begin with one reachable community whose relevant materials can be used appropriately. Research will establish which character traits matter, which interactions people would pay for, and what feels inaccurate or uncomfortable. The launch market depends on community access and partnership resources; demand is not assumed to be identical across regions.

### 3. User needs

**Users want an identity they love to take part in their lives in a familiar way—not simply another notification from a utility.**

A wake-up message can use the character's established nickname, tone, and signature phrases. Reminders can feel gentle, direct, humorous, or quiet. Notes can be captured through natural conversation instead of requiring the user to open a blank input field.

I want to combine my experience in art, visual expression, and branding into a coherent identity: appearance, voice, personality, and interaction detail. A character should not feel recognizable on day one and then revert to generic responses. Users should be able to refine its expression and preserve the characterization they value.

Emotional value needs dependable functionality. However appealing a character is, unreliable reminders or lost notes undermine long-term use. The product must earn both emotional recognition and practical trust.

### 4. Market & competitors

**Compete for people who love a character and want its help every day, rather than treating a new avatar as sufficient differentiation from character-chat products.**

Alternatives include voice assistants, reminder and note-taking tools, and AI companions with character creation and voice configuration. Character.AI already offers character and voice customization, so this capability alone is not an unserved market.

The comparison must cover the whole experience: nuanced characterization, consistency over time, adjustable forms of address and interaction, and whether these settings carry through wake-ups, reminders, and notes. Setup effort and the cost of voice and sustained use also matter.

The proposed advantages are detailed character configuration, usable everyday functions, and distribution through fan communities and creators. Actual use and payment must establish whether that combination is competitive.

### 5. Business model

**Attract fan communities with a clear demonstration of a favorite identity in everyday life, then monetize customization, continued use, or licensed character content.**

We can test a free trial followed by a subscription, or sell a paid version or themed package directly through a complete demo. Pricing for advanced voice, deeper customization, persistent context, and licensed content should reflect operating costs and user preferences. We should not assume users want to pay separately for every feature.

Initial distribution can come from anime and fan-community creators. Promotional content should show exactly how the character wakes someone up, reminds them, and remembers things for them, making the experience understandable within seconds. Fixed promotional fees, conversion-based revenue sharing, and licensed collaborations are options to discuss; none is confirmed.

The product warrants testing for rapid spread within a community: someone sees a beloved character, customizes it, shares their version, and attracts like-minded users. Channel, licensing, content, voice, and model costs must still be measured to determine whether attention produces payment and sustained use.

### 6. Validation plan

**Use working demos of a favorite character waking someone up to test whether fans progress from watching to customization, daily use, and payment.**

Begin with original or properly authorized character material and recruit within one community through a small campaign. Track views, trial entry, completed character setups, and real use of reminders and notes.

Alongside day-7 and day-30 retention and payment, evaluate whether users feel the character is recognizable, which details matter, whether they share it, and whether referred users continue using it. Comparing a standard and character-based version of the same function can help isolate the value of customization.

Set campaign budgets and review points beforehand. If videos attract attention but use does not persist, improve the experience. If use is sustained but acquisition costs are too high, revisit the audience or pricing. Short-term attention alone is not success.

### 7. Execution plan

**Build one complete, consistent experience from character setup to daily wake-ups, reminders, and notes before expanding the character library or audience.**

Start with the character profile and setup flow: users choose or supply usable imagery, specify a name, personality, phrases, and interaction preferences, then preview and confirm. Preserve versions of user-edited settings so the character does not drift further away over time.

Engineering should first evaluate mobile notifications, background behavior, voice, and note storage, then deliver a working version on one platform. The first release prioritizes recognizable traits and reliable everyday tasks; richer presentation and customization can follow.

I will lead character experience, visual expression, community research, demos, and creator relationships. The team will implement mobile, backend, voice, and configuration capabilities. Each review should assess character quality, reliability, cost, and channel results together.

### 8. Support needed

**Connect editable character profiles to reliable mobile utilities, and support one capped-budget distribution experiment in a focused community.**

Engineering support is needed for mobile and backend development, voice, and character context, including profile editing, consistent expression, reminders, notes, and user-data management. Resources include model, voice, storage, and demo-production budgets.

Public figures, commercial IP, and real-person materials require workable authorization and usage arrangements. The initial trial can use original or already permitted characters. Distribution support should establish partners, experiment budgets, and measurement.

I will lead product and market development. Staffing, duration, and amounts should be agreed after choosing the first community and experience scope. No celebrity or character will be presented as an available product before rights and resources are confirmed.

## Direction 2: Turn industry knowledge into Agents that produce content, identify opportunities, and execute work

### 1. Product idea

**Start with an aesthetics-industry Agent that researches specific products, produces professional materials, and studies target institutions, while exploring higher-value custom Agents for other industries.**

The standard product organizes product information, content production, institution research, business leads, sales support, and after-sales knowledge into workflows. Users specify a product, audience, content purpose, or target customer type and receive sourced, reviewable outputs they can use in their work—without rebuilding the context in every chat.

Custom services address a company's own processes and systems through diagnosis, solution design, development, integration, acceptance, and maintenance. One project might connect document retrieval, sales-material preparation, and customer follow-up, rather than deliver only a question-and-answer interface.

The two routes need separate validation: the industry product must identify repeatable needs; custom work must solve a defined customer's problem. Technology may be reused, but sales, cost accounting, and delivery scope should remain distinct.

### 2. Target users

**First serve institutions, brands, and practitioner teams that need a steady supply of professional content and qualified business opportunities.**

Institutional content and operations staff need to turn product and service information into articles, short-video scripts, storyboards, and campaign materials, reducing repeated research and revisions.

Consultation and sales staff need accurate product descriptions, suitability information, limitations, and FAQs to prepare for customer conversations. Clinical decisions remain with qualified professionals.

Product brands, distributors, and business-development teams need to identify relevant institutions, organize public business contact channels, prepare tailored introductions, and track opportunities.

Institution and team leaders want to reduce duplicated content, documentation, and commercial research effort while managing actual staff usage and outputs.

Custom Agents target companies in other industries with established processes, accountable owners, and budgets. The audience is businesses with real work to deliver, not consumers seeking clinical advice.

### 3. User needs

**Help practitioners retrieve reliable detail about a specific product, produce material that can pass review, and identify institutions worth approaching.**

The content system would use a product-level knowledge base, not merely a larger collection of general industry articles. If someone mentions “Filorga,” for example, it should first identify the exact product, model, market version, and available documentation, then retrieve descriptions, suitability information, limitations, and evidence. Questions about age groups or product combinations require relevant sources and professional review; the brand name alone cannot justify a conclusion.

This material can become educational copy, image cards, video scripts, storyboards, and sales collateral. Image workflows should manage licensed assets, brand templates, product labels, and consistent style. Real cases require authorization, and simulated effects must be labeled. The intended advantage comes from source quality, workflows, and review—not a blanket claim that general-purpose models know nothing about aesthetics.

Acquisition workflows would research public institutional information by region, type, and business profile; organize business channels; qualify prospects; draft outreach materials; and manage replies. Other industries' custom projects should diagnose repeated work and gaps between systems.

### 4. Market & competitors

**Demonstrate that this system is more reliable and requires less revision than directly prompting a general-purpose model on specific product-content and business-development tasks.**

Content alternatives include general models, generation tools, industry databases, human editors, and content agencies. Acquisition alternatives include business-information tools, CRM systems, commercial research, and human sales teams. Custom projects compete with automation tools and software services.

Four capabilities are proposed: traceable product-level information, updateable knowledge, consistent review across text, image, and video outputs, and research and outreach results that enter real work. The knowledge base must actually be built; planned information assets cannot be presented as an existing moat.

Evaluate the system and a general model on the same tasks with the same available sources. Qualified reviewers should blindly assess factual errors, omissions, editing effort, and time. This separates the value of better information from the system's own contribution and identifies what customers will pay for.

### 5. Business model

**Charge subscriptions and implementation fees for the industry product, and project fees tied to explicit deliverables for custom Agents.**

Standard plans could vary by content and institution-research modules, seats, and reasonable usage allowances. Importing an institution's knowledge, brand assets, or existing systems may require a separate implementation fee. Image, video, and large research jobs have costs and should not be offered without limits.

Custom quotes cover process diagnosis, design, development, integration, testing, training, and agreed maintenance, with staged payments and acceptance. Higher prices must be supported by business value, complexity, and engineering effort—not simply by calling the product an Agent.

Initial prospects should come from my industry contacts, AI consulting clients, and businesses the team can reach. Content and research demos show concrete outputs; paid pilots establish whether customers will fund the workflow on an ongoing basis.

### 6. Validation plan

**Use real product-content tasks, real institutional research, and one paid custom pilot to test professional quality, acquisition value, and delivery capability separately.**

Practitioners should provide common questions and usable source material for content experiments. Compare factual accuracy, citations, editing effort, review acceptance, and production time for copy, images, and scripts. Establish deliverability before measuring ongoing use and subscription intent.

Limit acquisition experiments by geography, institution type, and volume. Verify public information, business channels, and prospect fit. Use reviewed, permitted contact channels to measure substantive replies and subsequent business progress, while recording opt-out requests and cost. Collection and send counts alone are not acquisition success.

For custom work, choose one customer with an accountable owner, budget, and agreed acceptance criteria for a small pilot. Compare payment, outcomes, support effort, and maintenance cost before deciding which route to expand.

### 7. Execution plan

**Build a trustworthy product-information base and one content workflow first, then add a bounded, auditable business-research and outreach process.**

The content flow is: import usable sources → label product, version, date, and provenance → retrieve relevant information → draft copy or scripts → professional review → produce image and video materials → save reusable templates. Missing or conflicting information must be marked for confirmation rather than converted into a confident conclusion.

The acquisition flow is: specify region and institution type → research permitted public sources → extract institution-published business email, phone, or partnership channels → preserve provenance, deduplicate, and classify → match prospects to the customer's offering → draft tailored outreach → human review → contact through permitted channels → record replies, follow-up, and opt-outs. WeChat engagement should use published partnership routes, inbound requests, or consented enterprise channels—not bulk collection of private phone numbers and friend requests.

Other industries' custom projects follow diagnosis, quoting, prototyping, integration, acceptance, and maintenance. I will lead industry and customer work, product and content standards, sales, and review. The team will own engineering, integrations, permissions, and operational records.

### 8. Support needed

**Build updateable industry knowledge, multimodal content workflows, and reliable business integrations, while defining delivery rules for custom projects together.**

Engineering needs include source processing, retrieval, text/image/video generation, public institution research, CRM or business-system integration, review controls, permissions, and logs. Budgets are needed for models, image/video generation, hosting, and any necessary data services.

Industry work requires verifiable, usable documentation and qualified review. My experience can identify problems and guide content standards, but it cannot replace professional review for every product. The team's consulting experience can support quoting, scope changes, acceptance, and maintenance.

Choose one defined task and pilot customer before agreeing on engineering schedules and budgets. Avoid expanding the standard product and custom service without limits at the same time, or every customer's different requirements will erode delivery boundaries.

## Direction 3: Tell your story, generate a visual novel, and share it with others

### 1. Product idea

**Speak a story, submit a chat or article, and add a few character photos to generate a personal visual novel with characters, artwork, and branching choices.**

The system first extracts the main characters, relationships, and events, asking the user to confirm identities and facts to preserve. Main-character sprites use authorized photos and agreed settings. Supporting characters may come from original or licensed platform NPC assets, so users do not need a photo for everyone.

It then generates an outline, chapters, scene art, dialogue, and branches, assembling a playable game. Users can revisit the original story or explore fictional outcomes of different decisions—for example, another way of communicating in a relationship or choosing a career path.

We want to test expanding roughly 1,000–5,000 Chinese characters of input into a longer story. A 30,000–50,000-character demo is a target to test, not a delivered capability. Quality, cost, and waiting time must be evaluated, distinguishing all-branch text from the length of one playthrough. The value is a complete game, not word count alone.

### 2. Target users

**Attract ordinary people who have a story they want others to enter, then make playing a friend's creation a natural starting point for becoming a creator.**

Users need no scriptwriting, illustration, or game-development experience; they can simply tell their story. Early demos can cover relationships, breakups, job searches, campus life, and turning points, helping different people recognize that their experiences can become games.

The platform has two connected roles: creators submit material, generate, and share; players follow a link into someone else's story and then try creating their own. One person can move between both roles.

Initial acquisition would use complete demos, my content channels, relevant communities, and creator partnerships. The reach of these channels has not been validated. The US market requires separate testing of English narrative, art preferences, pricing, and sharing habits.

### 3. User needs

**Let first-time users turn their own experiences into a complete playable game for free, then earn opportunities to create again by sharing.**

The first experience should ask for only three things: tell or upload a story, supply photos for the main characters they want represented, and confirm the extracted cast and basic settings. The platform assists with supporting characters, scenes, and expansion. The free first work should have a clear scope and a complete experience—not merely a preview that becomes playable only after payment.

Users can play the result themselves or share it with friends. When others genuinely play, the creator can earn credits under defined rules. Enough credits unlock a second story. Players who enjoy the experience can move from playing to creating their own first work.

My experience with diaries, film, and AI-assisted narrative games would inform character detail, personal meaning, and choice design. Relationship and career branches are fiction, not predictions of real people's reactions or real-world outcomes.

### 4. Market & competitors

**Make writing, character art, game assembly, and sharing one smooth experience, with each finished work becoming an entry point for the next user.**

Alternatives include AI writing, image generation, interactive-story products, and visual-novel engines. Users can combine these tools, but must manage characters, branches, assets, and execution themselves. The actual effort required to finish a work still needs direct comparison.

The proposed distinction has two layers: turning personal material into a visual novel, and making the finished game immediately shareable so players can become creators. Characters should match the agreed settings, choices should change the story, and shared links should open something genuinely playable.

Credits alone are not a competitive advantage. We need to compare finished quality, creation completion, generation cost, and real participation after sharing to establish whether people want to stay in this experience.

### 5. Business model

**The first personal game is free; later games use credits earned through valid participation or purchased, while membership provides an agreed ongoing creation allowance.**

When independent players meet a defined playtime or completion threshold on a shared game, its creator earns credits. Players may also earn capped onboarding credits for activities such as completing their first experience, then try creating. Recipients, amounts, and generation costs require testing; the same play event must not generate unlimited repeated rewards.

Sufficient credits allow another creation. Users who do not want to wait or want more works can buy credits or membership. The earlier idea of about $5 per additional story can serve as a single-purchase comparison against credits and membership; no final exchange rate is set.

The loop is: create a free personal game → share it → others genuinely play → earn credits → create again; players enjoy it → make their own game → share again. This warrants testing for self-propagation, but free works and rewards consume generation resources. Budgets, allowances, and valid-activity rules must be designed together.

### 6. Validation plan

**Test whether people can finish their first game and, by sharing it, attract new users who play, create, and pay.**

Build complete relationship and job-search demos showing source material and playable results. Then invite people to contribute their own stories by voice, chat, or article. Observe character confirmation, first-generation completion, and tolerance for waiting.

Product metrics include generation success, character consistency, play depth, and branch experience. Distribution metrics include sharing, valid new players, player-to-creator conversion, and second creations. Revenue metrics include credit purchases, membership or one-off conversion, and the free and reward costs incurred before those payments.

A small controlled experiment can compare sharing without rewards against sharing with capped rewards. Exclude repeated self-play and determine whether incentives bring new participants rather than meaningless activity. Sustained growth is an outcome to test, not an assumption.

### 7. Execution plan

**First connect storytelling, character confirmation, generation, play, and sharing; then add credits and payments based on genuine play activity.**

Creation supports speech transcription and article or chat import. The system proposes principal characters and relationships; users confirm them and link photos, while NPC assets cover supporting characters without source images. After outline confirmation, chapters, sprites, scenes, and branches are generated in stages, with visible progress and recovery from failures.

Shared links should let people start playing with minimal friction while respecting private access choices. Account steps can be introduced when needed to save progress, claim rewards, or create. Valid play triggers a reward record; creators see their balance and the credits required for another game. Private works should remain fully usable without forcing public sharing for basic functionality.

I will lead demos, narrative, art direction, experience, and content-led acquisition. The team will implement generation orchestration, character and branch state, the player, sharing, credits, and payments. Measure actual cost with a limited cohort before increasing free allowances or rewards.

### 8. Support needed

**Build both personal-game generation and a sharing-reward system, so a free first experience can be reliably delivered, distributed, and accounted for.**

Generation requires voice input, text/image orchestration, character management, original or licensed NPC assets, a visual-novel player, progress tracking, and failure recovery. Distribution requires share links, valid-play records, a credit ledger, duplicate-claim prevention, payments, and membership.

Budgets must cover sample production, a small free-generation cohort, images and long-form text, storage, and experimental rewards, including retries and maintenance. User materials require appropriate usage consent, access controls, deletion, and user-directed sharing.

We should agree on the first-game specification, credit costs, valid-play conditions, reward budget, and membership allowances. Validate output quality and unit costs before the sharing loop; do not promise unlimited generation or rewards before those costs are known.

## Decisions to make together

The first steps are a distinctive, functional personal-AI demo; a sourced industry-task and business-research workflow sample; and a visual-novel prototype that supports free creation, shared play, and measurement of subsequent behavior.

I would like to discuss which direction can reach its first real users, which capabilities the team can contribute, and how a bounded budget can produce evidence to continue or stop. These are alternatives to choose from, not a commitment to build all three fully at once. Resources, timing, and partnership terms would be agreed separately.
